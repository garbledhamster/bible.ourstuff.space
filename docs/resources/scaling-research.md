# Scaling the concept — research notes

## A method that transfers

The scale loop below draws on startup validation, production operations, software reliability, game development, and replication of proven programs. The shared pattern is an interpretation of those sources. Each project needs its own unit, evidence, budget, and limits.

1. **Choose what will grow and what must still work.** Name the unit of value and current and next levels. Orders per week, tenants served, peak concurrent matches, and levels shipped per month are different scale problems. A new region or operator also changes the context in which the concept has to work. Name the parts that make the outcome possible and the parts the next operator can adapt. WHO and ExpandNet place a proven innovation, its adopting organization, its operating environment, and support capacity in the same plan. Their guide recommends simplification while retaining essential components. [WHO and ExpandNet, Nine steps for developing a scaling-up strategy](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241500319)
2. **Look for proof that the next increment is worth serving.** For a paid product, use actual purchases, repeat purchases, or measurable commitments through the channel you plan to expand. For software or games, real adoption, successful task completion, or unprompted return play can supply evidence suited to the intended outcome. Separately estimate what each successful unit consumes in money, staff time, infrastructure, or support and what new fixed costs begin at the next tier. A simple single-offer break-even estimate divides fixed costs by positive contribution per unit, where contribution equals price less variable cost. This assumes a reasonable cost/price estimate over the relevant range. Recalculate at the next tier when costs, discounts, or demand change. Include the cash and staffing needed during the ramp even if the target tier looks viable. [Steve Blank, Search versus Execute](https://steveblank.com/2012/03/05/search-versus-execute/), [U.S. Small Business Administration, Break-Even Calculator](https://bec.www.sba.gov/breakevenpointcalculator)
3. **Measure the current constraint.** Map one unit from demand to completed value, including shared approvals, handoffs, and recurring support. Compare arrival and completion rates over the same interval. Inspect where work waits, how old it gets, which dependency limits successful output under representative peaks, and whether variation or rework consumes its capacity. Demand itself can be the constraint. Improve how the existing constraint works and align upstream effort to it. Add capacity if it still limits the intended outcome, then measure again because the constraint can move. [Goldratt Marketing, Theory of Constraints](https://www.toc-goldratt.com/en/about-toc), [Theory of Constraints Institute, Five Focusing Steps](https://www.tocinstitute.org/five-focusing-steps.html), [AWS Well-Architected, Load test your workload](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel_adapt_to_changes_load_tested_adapt.html)
4. **Make the proven unit repeatable and contain failures.** Record the minimum procedure, owner, interfaces, quality checks, and what happens when the work fails. Ask another operator to run it without the original team's special help. Toyota describes getting work right by hand, improving it, then automating and detecting or stopping on abnormalities. When repeated units coordinate, make dependencies explicit and separate pieces if independence addresses a measured limit. DORA treats changes and testing without cross-team coordination as the outcome and describes architectures with different costs at different scales. AWS uses cells or other isolation boundaries to bound the effect of an outage. A business can apply the idea by keeping an established delivery path available during a new site pilot. [Toyota, Toyota Production System](https://global.toyota/en/company/vision-and-philosophy/production-system/), [DORA, Loosely coupled teams](https://dora.dev/capabilities/loosely-coupled-teams/), [AWS Well-Architected, Bulkhead architectures](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel_fault_isolation_use_bulkhead.html)
5. **Test one bounded increment and decide from the results.** Record the baseline, target, expected result, decision owner, duration, representative cohort or workload, and a few limits covering user outcome, quality, and operator capacity or cost. Test the end-to-end flow at expected peaks and for long enough that recurring work and backlogs show up. Decide in advance which thresholds allow expansion, which call for more evidence, and which trigger a halt. Be explicit about how to stop new demand and recover commitments or state already created. For reversible software changes the response may be routing traffic back. For orders already taken or irreversible data changes, plan a separate recovery or repair path. Compare results with the prediction and baseline. Expand one step, hold and change the plan, or stop and recover. Record what changed and start again from the new baseline. [The Deming Institute, PDSA Cycle](https://deming.org/explore/pdsa/), [Google SRE Workbook, Canarying Releases](https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/), [AWS Well-Architected, Load test your workload](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/rel_adapt_to_changes_load_tested_adapt.html)

## Evidence from games

- **Scale the experience and the content pipeline.** Riot’s Swarm team spawned hundreds of minions, measured the largest performance bottleneck, and repeated. Per-agent pathfinding became a limit as moving minions interacted, so Riot switched to a shared flow field and simplified movement. Riot also put wave parameters in editor-authored data, with efficient execution in the game engine. The performance change and authoring change address separate constraints. [Riot Games, The Tech Behind Swarm](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/the-tech-behind-swarm)
- **Scale the audience infrastructure.** Riot simulated plausible player requests through interacting VALORANT platform services and reported a test with two million simulated players against one test shard. Riot tested gameplay server performance separately. The relevant lesson for a smaller game is to derive a test workload from real player actions and try the full path through login, matchmaking, and persisted results before admitting the next cohort. [Riot Games, Scalability and Load Testing for VALORANT](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/scalability-and-load-testing-valorant)
- **Limit access during the pilot.** Steam Playtest lets developers grant access in batches and take the test offline. This can support a cohort playtest before opening additional places. A game still needs its own success measure, feedback mechanism, device coverage, and support limits. [Valve, Steam Playtest](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/playtest)

## Suggested chapter exercise

One small plan should fit this sentence. “We have evidence that **[audience]** gets **[outcome]** from **[unit]**. We will go from **[baseline]** to **[next tier]** by changing **[measured constraint]**. **[Owner]** will run **[cohort or workload]** through **[date or evidence window]** and check **[outcome, quality, and cost/capacity limits]**. If **[stop condition]** occurs, we will **[contain and recover]**. On **[review date]** we will expand, hold, or stop based on **[evidence]**.”

For example, an order service might aim to grow from 20 to 30 fulfilled orders per week. Buying ads would raise intake, so first verify demand already reaches packing when the queue grows. A second operator follows the packing procedure on one capped batch. The owner checks on-time completion against the current baseline and labor per order against the available margin, then pauses new intake and completes accepted orders if either limit is breached. In a co-op game, the same exercise might cover 50 to 100 peak concurrent players, with match completion, queue time, and cost per match in place of order metrics. The specific figures are illustrative. For replicated sites, include the local operator, required shared services, and which parts the new site can adapt while still meeting the same quality standard.
